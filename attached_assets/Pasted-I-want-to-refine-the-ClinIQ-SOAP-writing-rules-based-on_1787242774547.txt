RULE 1 — WRITE IN THE TREATING CLINICIAN'S VOICE

The SOAP note is the clinician's medical documentation. It must not sound like an AI observer summarizing what a provider said.

Avoid distancing/observer language such as:

“the provider stated”
“the clinician recommended”
“was described as low”
“was described as elevated”
“the stated goal”
“the discussed goal”
“the provider reviewed”

Clinical interpretations should be documented directly when supported by the encounter.

Examples:

Avoid: “Testosterone was described as low.”

Prefer: “Testosterone is low on current laboratory testing.”

Avoid: “ApoB was below the discussed goal.”

Prefer: “ApoB is 90, within goal for her current cardiovascular risk category.”

Normal clinical documentation phrases such as “risks and benefits were discussed,” “treatment options were reviewed,” or “patient elected...” are acceptable. The goal is not to eliminate passive voice globally; it is to eliminate language that makes the note sound like a third-party narrator.

RULE 2 — FINAL PLAN PRECEDENCE / RECONCILE EVOLVING DECISIONS

The transcript is conversational and clinical decisions may evolve throughout the encounter. The provider may initially consider one treatment and later modify, replace, combine, clarify, or abandon it.

The SOAP writer must read the entire transcript and determine the provider's final stated treatment decision for each problem.

Later definitive decisions supersede earlier tentative or exploratory decisions unless the provider clearly intends both interventions to remain active.

Do not simply collect every treatment statement made during the encounter and treat all of them as simultaneous active plans.

Pay particular attention to later statements such as:

“actually...”
“instead...”
“let's do...”
“we'll do...”
“I'm going to send...”
“I'll put that in...”
“I'm going to put X in there too...”
“we'll switch...”
“we'll hold off on...”
“never mind...”
other language indicating that the treatment plan has been finalized or changed.

Before writing the final A&P, internally reconcile each medication/treatment into its final encounter state: continue, start, increase/decrease, combine, discontinue, defer, consider later, or unresolved.

If the transcript genuinely does not establish the final plan, preserve that uncertainty rather than choosing one.

This rule is particularly important for medication dose changes, route changes, compounded medications, prescriptions, and decisions that are revisited near the end of the encounter.

RULE 3 — DIAGNOSE CONDITIONS, NOT INTERVENTIONS

Assessment & Plan diagnoses/problems should represent actual clinical conditions, symptoms, risk states, or legitimate problems being assessed/managed.

Do not create a separate diagnosis/problem merely because a medication, supplement, counseling topic, test, or intervention needs somewhere to go.

Examples of things that generally should NOT become standalone diagnoses solely because they were discussed:

“Cardiac health support”
“Systemic inflammation support”
“Berberine therapy”
“Supplement management”
“Medication reconciliation”
“Nutritional supplement management”

Instead, place the intervention under the most clinically relevant diagnosis.

Examples:

omega-3 / CoQ10 / cardiovascular counseling → dyslipidemia or appropriate cardiovascular-risk problem
berberine / metabolic nutrition counseling → insulin resistance, prediabetes, metabolic dysfunction, etc., when supported
iron supplementation → iron deficiency when supported
hormone interventions → appropriate menopausal/hormonal problem

If counseling or a supplement does not justify its own diagnosis and does not clearly belong under another problem, preserve it in the Care Plan rather than manufacturing a diagnosis.

Do not omit an intervention simply because it does not warrant its own diagnosis. Preserve the information and place it appropriately.

RULE 4 — OPTIMIZATION ABNORMALITY IS NOT AUTOMATICALLY A DISEASE

ClinIQ may use optimized/functional targets that are intentionally narrower than conventional laboratory reference ranges. A laboratory value being outside a ClinIQ optimization target does not automatically establish an ICD-10 disease or deficiency diagnosis.

Distinguish between:

a value that is clinically optimized below/above the ClinIQ target,
a conventional abnormality,
and an actual diagnosed condition.

Do not convert “suboptimal” into “deficient” merely to create an ICD-10 diagnosis.

For example, if vitamin D is 36 and the clinician discusses an optimization goal of 60–80, document that it is below the desired optimization target if that reflects the encounter. Do not automatically diagnose vitamin D deficiency solely because it is below the ClinIQ target unless the provider diagnosed deficiency or the available clinical criteria support that diagnosis.

The same principle applies to hormone optimization, ferritin targets, metabolic markers, thyroid optimization, and other ClinIQ-specific targets.

IMPORTANT FIDELITY PRINCIPLE

The ClinIQ rules help the model organize and document clinical reasoning. They must not override what actually occurred during the encounter.

The raw transcript remains the primary source of truth for:

what the provider diagnosed,
what was prescribed,
what was changed,
what was continued,
what was stopped,
what was deferred,
and what counseling occurred.

The model may organize related information clinically, but it must not replace the provider's actual treatment decision with what it thinks the provider should have done.

Completeness is also critical: correcting diagnosis grouping must never cause a medication, supplement recommendation, counseling point, follow-up instruction, or other clinically meaningful item from the encounter to disappear.

During an encounter, the provider initially discussed increasing/continuing an estradiol patch and separately compounding topical testosterone. Later in the encounter, after discussing patch availability, the provider stated:

“I'm going to put your estrogen in there too.”

The generated SOAP continued to document a higher-strength estradiol patch plus separate compounded testosterone and failed to recognize that the later statement modified the final prescribing plan.

The updated prompt should teach the model to reconcile this type of evolving decision and represent the final treatment plan, rather than remaining anchored to an earlier proposal.
